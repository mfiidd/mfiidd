Model checking: what the simulator tells you

Prior predictive checks, posterior predictive checks and simulation-based calibration all push parameters through the same simulator. What changes between them is where the parameters come from, and that determines what each check can and cannot see.

Three checks, one simulator

Only the source of θ changes

Everything on this page runs the model the course session runs. A parameter vector θ = (R0, Dinf, ρ) goes into a deterministic SIR model, which returns daily incidence, and a Poisson observation model turns incidence into reported counts.

/* transmission */ dS/dt = −β S I / N     dI/dt = β S I / N − ν I     β = R0 / Dinf,   ν = 1 / Dinf
/* observation */ yi ~ Poisson( ρ · [ S(i−1) − S(i) ] )

The island starts at S = 279, I = 2, R = 3, so N = 284. Day 1 is 13 August 1971, the day the ship landed. There are 59 days of counts and 312 reported cases in total. Hold on to that last pair of numbers.

The three checks differ in one line:

prior predictive       θ(i) ~ p(θ)  →  yrep,(i) ~ p(y | θ(i))
posterior predictive   θ(i) ~ p(θ | y)  →  yrep,(i) ~ p(y | θ(i))
SBC                      θ(i) ~ p(θ)  →  y(i) ~ p(y | θ(i))  →  fit, then rank θ(i)

These are steps 2 and 5 of the workflow in Gelman et al. (2020). The loop matters more than any single check: each failure tells you which part of the model to change, and you go round again.

Vague on the parameter is not vague on the outcome

The push-forward through the model is the whole point

A flat prior on R0 looks like an admission of ignorance. Push it through the SIR model and it stops being flat. The map from R0 to attack rate saturates: once R0 is above about 4 the epidemic infects essentially the whole island, so most of a wide prior piles up on the same outcome. The map to peak timing compresses the other way, because the growth rate β − ν is (R0 − 1) / Dinf and the time to the peak falls roughly like 1 / (R0 − 1).

Posterior predictive, and why it flatters the model

Same simulator, parameters from p(θ | y), and one data set doing two jobs

The posterior predictive distribution replaces draws from the prior with draws from the posterior. Nothing else changes. The comparison is no longer "could this model produce anything like an epidemic" but "does this fitted model produce data that look like the data we have".

There is a catch the session does not state. The posterior was pulled towards y by y itself, and the replicates come from that posterior. Any statistic the fit was able to chase has already been chased, so the observed value sits closer to the middle of the replicate distribution than a fresh data set would. The check is optimistic by construction.

The slider makes that visible. Fit to the first k days only, then look at how the same posterior predictive interval performs on the days inside the fitting window and on the days outside it.

Predictive p-values, and what they are not

Pick a statistic, compare the observed value against its replicated distribution

A visual check needs a person to look at it. A predictive p-value reduces the comparison to one number. Choose a summary statistic T, compute it on every replicate, and record where the observed value falls:

pB = Pr( T(yrep) ≥ T(y) | y )   ≈   #{ i : T(yrep,(i)) ≥ T(y) }number of replicates

Near 0.5 the observed value is unremarkable under the model. Near 0 or near 1 it sits in a tail, and the sign tells you the direction of the failure. Switch statistics below. One fitted model, one data set, and the answer runs the whole length of the scale.

The two that pass are worth more attention than the four that fail, because both are statistics a reader might reasonably have chosen in advance.

  • Day of the first case passes because it carries almost no information. Any epidemic that gets going at all reports its first case within a day or two, whatever R0 is, so the statistic cannot distinguish a model that is right from one that is not.
  • Mean day of cases passes for a worse reason. Two errors cancel. The fitted single wave peaks around day — against an observed peak on day 7, which on its own would push the replicate mean day too late. The observed second wave drags the observed mean day out to —, and the replicate median lands at —. The statistic agrees because the model is late and the data are bimodal, and it would agree just as well if neither were true.

A statistic the model reproduces is weak evidence. A statistic it misses points at the missing mechanism. Choose summaries that could plausibly fail, and report the ones that did.

Why these are not calibrated p-values

A frequentist p-value is uniformly distributed under the null hypothesis, which is what makes a fixed threshold mean the same thing every time. A posterior predictive p-value is not, and the reason is the double use of the data. The replicates are drawn from p(θ | y), a distribution that has already been pulled towards y. The observed statistic is therefore closer to the centre of its own replicate distribution than a genuinely fresh data set would be, so the p-value clusters around 0.5 rather than spreading uniformly. The check is conservative: it under-reports problems.

Meng (1994) made this precise, showing that under the model the posterior predictive p-value satisfies Pr(pB ≤ α) ≤ 2α. It can be twice as far into the tail as a uniform p-value would be at the same nominal level, but no further. Gelman (2013) gives worked examples where the distribution is a long way from uniform in both directions. Two consequences follow.

  • Do not threshold. There is no 0.05 here. A value of 0.03 does not mean the model is rejected and a value of 0.2 does not mean it is fine.
  • Read the direction, not the magnitude. pB near 1 for peak day says the model peaks late, every time. That statement survives the calibration problem. "Significantly late" does not.

Prior predictive p-values in the sense of Box (1980) are uniform under the prior predictive distribution, because the data are used only once. They are also far less useful, as the panel above shows: a vague prior makes them pass whatever you feed them.

A ceiling no parameter can clear

312 reported cases in a population of 284

The checks above all say the model misses. They do not say whether the problem is the parameters or the structure. For this data set the answer is available before any fitting, from a conservation argument.

Divide the susceptible equation by the recovered equation. The transmission terms cancel and the result depends on R0 alone. Integrating from the start of the outbreak to the end, where I has returned to zero, gives the classical final size relation:

dSdR = − R0N S   →   S(∞) = S(0) · exp( − R0N [ N − S(∞) − R(0) ] )
/* cumulative infections over the whole outbreak */ C(∞) = S(0) − S(∞)  <  S(0) = 279

The recovered compartment and the basic reproduction number are both written R by long convention. The compartment is R and the reproduction number is R0.

Everyone who is ever infected in a single SIR wave leaves the susceptible compartment once and never returns, so the cumulative number of infections cannot exceed S(0) = 279 no matter what R0 and Dinf are. Reporting only shrinks that further, since the expected number of reported cases is ρ · C(∞) and ρ ≤ 1. The observed total is 312.

Strictly, "impossible" is one word too strong, and the page computes the correction. The Poisson observation model has variance equal to its mean, so a total of 312 could arise as an upward fluctuation around an expectation of 279. That requires ρ = 1, an R0 large enough to infect every susceptible, and then a draw — standard deviations above the mean. The chip above gives the probability of the last step alone.

The right-hand panel shows why chasing that configuration is hopeless anyway. Press push it as far as it goes. The model puts — cases on the first day and is finished within a week. It buys the total by destroying the shape.

A parameter problem or a structure problem. A parameter problem moves when you move the parameters. This one does not, so no prior, no sampler and no observation model will fix it. Reported cases exceed the population because islanders were infected twice, which a single SIR wave cannot represent. Changing Poisson to negative binomial widens the interval around a single-wave trajectory and makes the miss look less surprising. It does not add a second wave. The SEITL model in the next session adds a temporary immune state that drains back into susceptible, which does.

Simulation-based calibration

Checking the inference rather than the model

Predictive checks compare the model against one data set. Simulation-based calibration asks a different question: given that the model is exactly right, does the fitting procedure return the correct posterior? It works from a self-consistency property. If θ is drawn from the prior and y is simulated from θ, then θ is an ordinary draw from the posterior p(θ | y). It is exchangeable with the fitted posterior samples, so its rank among L of them is uniform on 0, 1, …, L (Talts et al. 2018).

/* repeated many times */ θtrue ~ p(θ)  →  y ~ p(y | θtrue)  →  θ(1..L) ~ p(θ | y)
r = #{ l : θ(l) < θtrue }   /* uniform on {0, …, L} if the inference is correct */

The three failures have three signatures, and they are legible once you know that the rank counts how many posterior draws fall below the truth. A posterior that is too narrow misses the truth on one side or the other, so ranks pile up at both ends and the histogram is U-shaped. A posterior that is too wide brackets the truth too comfortably, so ranks cluster in the middle and the histogram arches. A posterior that is shifted puts too many draws on one side, and the histogram slopes.

Modern practice reads the empirical CDF of the ranks against uniform instead of binning them. The difference plot has no bin-width choice, and a band can be drawn on it.

The band is pointwise: each rank threshold is checked at the 95% level on its own, so with 100 thresholds a calibrated run is expected to stray outside it somewhere. Judge the shape of the excursion rather than counting crossings. Simultaneous bands, which do control the overall rate, are given by Säilynoja, Bürkner and Vehtari (2022).

What is simulated here. Repeating a NUTS fit of the SIR model several hundred times is beyond a web page, so this figure uses a conjugate stand-in: θ ~ Normal(0, 1) with eight observations yj ~ Normal(θ, 1), whose exact posterior is Normal(m, s2) with s2 = 1/9 and m = (Σ yj)/9. The correct posterior is therefore known, and the sliders corrupt it deliberately by scaling s and shifting m. Everything else, including the ranking and the band, is exactly what SBC does on a real model.
SBC does not check the model. The data it fits were generated by the model, so a structurally wrong model that is fitted correctly passes SBC with a flat histogram. The SIR fit on this page would pass. That is the division of labour: SBC audits the inference, predictive checks audit the model, and neither substitutes for the other.

Common questions

The ones that come up in the practical

What is the difference between a prior predictive check and just plotting my prior?

Plotting the prior shows you what you believe about a parameter. A prior predictive check shows you what that belief implies about data, after the model has transformed it. The transformation is where the surprises are: Uniform(1, 20) on R0 is flat on the parameter and heavily concentrated on "the whole island is infected within a week" on the outcome.

My posterior predictive check passes. Is the model right?

No. It means the fitted model reproduces the statistics you chose to look at, on the data it was fitted to. This page has two statistics that pass on a model that cannot possibly be right, one because it carries no information and one because two errors cancel. A statistic the model misses is worth far more than one it matches.

Why can I not use p < 0.05 here?

Because the data were used twice, once to form the posterior and again to compute the statistic, the p-value is not uniform under the model. It concentrates around 0.5, so the check is conservative and a fixed threshold does not mean what it means in a frequentist test. Read the direction of the failure, and read it alongside the plots.

How many SBC replicates do I need?

Enough that the band is narrow enough to see the deviation you care about, which in practice is a few hundred for an obvious failure and closer to a thousand for a subtle one. Set the width to 0.9 with no bias and drag the replicates slider: at 50 replicates — of the 100 rank thresholds fall outside the band, and at 1000 replicates — of them do.

SBC passed but my fit to real data is terrible. Why?

SBC only ever fits data that the model itself generated, so it certifies that the sampler recovers parameters when the model is true. It is silent about whether the model is true. A correct sampler on a model that cannot produce two waves gives you a correct posterior for a useless model.

Do I need to run SBC every time?

No. It costs hundreds of fits, so it belongs to model development rather than to daily use: a new likelihood, a new parameterisation, a custom sampler, or a pipeline whose results you intend to rely on repeatedly. For an established model in well-tested software, predictive checks and the usual convergence diagnostics catch most of what goes wrong.

The check failed. Do I change the observation model or the dynamics?

Ask whether the failure is about spread or about shape. If the replicates have the right shape but are too tight around it, the observation model is the cheap fix, and a negative binomial is the usual first move. If the replicates have the wrong shape, as on this page, no observation model will help, because it changes how the gap is scored rather than what the model can produce.

Why does the prior predictive distribution not use the fitted reporting rate?

Because at that stage there is no fit. The prior predictive distribution uses the prior on every parameter, including ρ ~ Uniform(0, 1), which is why its intervals are so much wider than the posterior predictive ones in the same figure.
