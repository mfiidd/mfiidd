Observation models: a likelihood is a distance

Naming an observation model fixes a rule for scoring how far each observation sits from the prediction. The rule decides how wide your posterior comes out, where its centre lands, and what happens when the model is wrong about the mean. This page draws the rule.

The negative log-likelihood, drawn as a distance

Fix the prediction, vary the observation, and read off what each family charges

Fitting maximises the log-likelihood, so it minimises the negative log-likelihood. Written out for one observation y against one prediction λ, that quantity is a function of how far y sits from λ. It is a distance in the loose sense that matters here: a number that is small when prediction and data agree and grows as they separate.

Poisson:   −log p(y | λ) = λ − y log λ + log y!
Negative binomial (mean μ, dispersion φ):   −log p(y | μ, φ) = −log Γ(y+φ) + log Γ(φ) + log y! − φ log φφ+μ − y log μφ+μ
Gaussian (fixed σ):   −log p(y | μ, σ) = (y − μ)²2σ² + log σ + ½ log 2π

The Gaussian one is a squared error divided by a constant. Fitting a mean with a Gaussian likelihood at fixed σ is least squares, exactly. Estimating σ as well does not change which mean wins, because the profile over σ leaves the residual sum of squares as the only term that depends on the mean. So least squares is an observation model, and it is the one that says the noise has the same size at every count.

Each curve has its own minimum subtracted, so the vertical axis reads as the extra log-likelihood cost of that observation against the best case for the same family. Subtracting is what makes the three comparable: the Poisson and negative binomial return a probability mass and the Gaussian a probability density, so their raw heights live on different scales and only differences within a family carry meaning. The session plots the raw values, which is fine for reading shapes and misleading if you compare heights across families.

Press Tristan da Cunha, day 7. The fitted single-wave model predicts about 5 cases that day and 47 were reported. Under Poisson that observation costs about 64 log-likelihood units against the best case for the same prediction. Under a negative binomial with φ = 0.5 it costs about 7, and under a Gaussian with σ = 5 about 35. One day is deciding the fit under Poisson and is merely a bad day under the negative binomial.

Switch the axis to vary the prediction to see the shape inference actually walks over. With y held at the observed value, the Poisson curve rises steeply on the left, because a prediction near zero makes a large count essentially impossible, and rises gently on the right. The Gaussian curve is a parabola, symmetric about y and the same width whether the counts are 3 or 300. The negative binomial is the flattest of the three. It lies below the Poisson everywhere on this axis and rises towards it as φ grows, and below the Gaussian too except at the far left, where a prediction near zero is costly under any count model.

Variance against the mean, and how to check it

Poisson forces one relationship. The data are free to disagree, and here they do

The Poisson has one parameter, so its spread is not a free choice. The negative binomial adds a second, and the variance becomes

Poisson:   Var = μ      negative binomial:   Var = μ + μ²/φ

As φ grows the second term vanishes and the Poisson is recovered. As φ falls the variance grows with the square of the mean, so the spread widens fastest exactly where the counts are largest. This is the parameterisation Julia's NegativeBinomial(r, p) reaches through r = φ and p = φ/(φ+μ), and the one written θ in MASS::glm.nb and k in much of the epidemiological literature. It is often called NB2, after the power of μ in the second term (Cameron and Trivedi, Regression Analysis of Count Data).

The distribution is also a Poisson whose rate is itself gamma distributed, with mean μ and shape φ, which is where the mixture reading comes from (Greenwood and Yule, 1920). That is the closest thing to a mechanism the negative binomial has: reporting intensity varies from day to day and the counts are Poisson given the intensity of the day.

Squared residuals are noisy estimates of variance one day at a time, so the larger diamonds average them within bins of similar fitted mean. Every bin sits above the Poisson line.

The ratio in the first chip is the check the session runs, and it comes out near 25 where the Poisson demands 1. Two different things push it up. Some is genuine observation noise, because two islanders infected in the same household are not independent draws and reporting was not uniform. Most of it here is that a single-wave SIR fitted to a two-wave outbreak is wrong about the mean, and everything it gets wrong about the mean lands in the residuals. The check tells you the Poisson is untenable. It does not tell you which of the two is responsible. The stochastic models page takes up the process noise half of the question, running a two-wave SEITL model at R0 = 10 on the same 59 days.

Notice what widening the interval does and does not fix. At φ = 0.5 most days fall inside the band, so the model has stopped calling the data impossible. The band is still centred on a curve with one peak, and the second wave from day 19 onwards still sits on the shoulder of a trajectory that has already burnt out. A wide interval around the wrong mean is a wide interval around the wrong mean.

What the choice does to the posterior

It widens the interval, and it moves the answer

The usual account is that a likelihood which is too tight makes the posterior overconfident. Every departure from the trajectory is scored as strong evidence, so the parameters get pinned down harder than the data warrant. That much is true and the figure shows it. The second effect is the one worth carrying away: the posterior mean moves as well.

The curves are marginal posteriors for R0 from the same SIR model fitted to the same 59 days, differing only in the observation model. The red curve is Poisson. The indigo curve is negative binomial at the φ you set.

The session's uniform prior for R0 also runs to 20 rather than 8, which changes nothing visible, because less than 10−15 of the posterior mass sits above R0 = 5 at any dispersion on the slider.

At φ = 0.5 the posterior mean for R0 moves from 1.84 to 2.66, and the standard deviation grows from 0.19 to 0.50. Those two means are 4.4 Poisson standard deviations apart. The two observation models do not merely disagree about the uncertainty. They disagree about the answer.

Why the mean moves

The misfit is concentrated in the first wave. Day 7 reported 47 cases against about 5 predicted, and the first eighteen days carry roughly nine tenths of the squared residual. The Poisson fit cannot raise its peak to reach those days, because a single SIR curve has one peak to spend and days 19 to 59 still carry 109 of the 312 reported cases. Raising R0 makes the epidemic burn out sooner, and the Poisson likelihood then gives those later observations almost no probability at all.

The negative binomial charges much less for missing a day, so the later observations stop vetoing a faster epidemic. The fit settles on a longer infectious period and a larger R0, and on a larger predicted total. Watch the reporting rate while you do this. Under both models it is pinned against the upper bound of its Uniform(0.1, 1.0) prior, because the model starts with 279 susceptibles and can never produce the 312 cases that were reported. A parameter stuck at the edge of its prior that still cannot close the gap is a structural failure, and no observation model repairs it.

An observation model will volunteer to explain a wrong mean

Two different worlds, one fitted dispersion, no way to tell them apart from φ alone

The dispersion parameter measures the scatter left over once the mean has been fitted as well as it can be. It has no way of knowing where that scatter came from. Here are two worlds where it comes from opposite places.

  • Noisy reporting. The mean is a single SIR wave, exactly the shape being fitted, and the counts are drawn from a negative binomial around it at a dispersion you set. The model is right about the mean and the data really are overdispersed.
  • Wrong mean. The counts are drawn from a Poisson, so the reporting is as clean as reporting gets, but the mean is two waves and the model being fitted has one. Nothing about the observation process is overdispersed.

Both datasets are then fitted with the same single-wave SIR and a negative binomial likelihood. The lower panel is the profile log-likelihood for φ, maximised over the reproduction number, infectious period and reporting rate at each value, with each curve shifted so its own maximum is zero.

Press matched. World 1 has genuinely overdispersed reporting at a true φ of about 1.4. World 2 has perfectly Poisson reporting around a mean the model cannot represent, so its true φ is infinite. Both come back with a fitted φ of 1.13, and the two profile curves peak in the same place. A small fitted φ is not evidence of messy surveillance. It is evidence of scatter, and a misspecified mean produces scatter.

Press no second wave at all to see the control. World 2 then is the model that generated it, the Poisson reporting is correctly specified, and the fitted φ runs off the top of the grid because no overdispersion is needed. Slide the second wave back up and φ collapses within a few steps. The structural error and the noise enter the likelihood through the same door.

This is why posterior predictive checks come before the choice of observation model. A check that looks at the shape of the residuals over time, rather than at their spread, separates the two cases: a misspecified mean leaves residuals that are correlated in time and change sign in runs, whereas genuine overdispersion leaves them scattered. Neither the fitted φ nor the variance-to-mean ratio can make that distinction, because both discard the time ordering.

Choosing the distance directly

Where this leads

Every figure on this page treats the negative log-likelihood as a rule for scoring the gap between prediction and data. Choosing Poisson over negative binomial over Gaussian is choosing between three such rules, and the choice was made by naming a distribution before seeing how it scored anything. If the rule is the object that matters, the distribution is a roundabout way of specifying it.

So you could pick the rule yourself. Simulate from the model, measure the distance between each simulation and the data with a function you wrote down, and keep the parameters whose simulations land close enough. That is Approximate Bayesian Computation, and it is the next session. It also buys something the likelihood approach cannot: for many models the likelihood cannot be written down at all, and a simulator is all there is.

Worth deciding before you get there: which features of the Tristan da Cunha data would you choose to match? The peak height, the timing, the total, the shape of the tail? Naming Poisson was a way of answering that question without being asked it.

Common questions

The ones that come up in the practical

How do I choose between Poisson and negative binomial?

Start with the negative binomial for surveillance counts and let the data pull φ upwards if no overdispersion is there. The cost of one extra parameter is small and the cost of a wrongly narrow posterior is not. Reach for Poisson when you have a reason to expect independent reporting at a stable rate, and check the variance against the mean rather than assuming it.

What does φ actually mean, and which parameterisation is it?

See the variance section for the parameterisation and the Julia mapping. The trap is that some software instead reports 1/φ, so check which way round your output runs before quoting a number.

My fitted φ is tiny. Is my surveillance system bad?

Not necessarily, and the fourth section is that question. A small φ says there is scatter around the fitted mean, and a mean that is structurally wrong produces scatter just as reliably as noisy reporting does. Check the residuals for runs and sign changes over time before you blame the data.

Is the φ here the same as the k in superspreading papers?

No. The k in Lloyd-Smith et al. (2005) is the dispersion of the offspring distribution, a property of transmission. The φ here is the dispersion of the reporting process around a predicted mean. Both are negative binomial dispersions and they answer different questions, so a small one does not imply the other.

Can I just use least squares?

You already are choosing an observation model if you do: least squares is the Gaussian likelihood at fixed σ. It assumes the noise is the same size at 3 cases and at 300, which for counts is usually wrong, and it puts probability on negative counts. It is defensible when counts are large and roughly constant in scale, and it is a poor default across an epidemic curve where the mean moves by two orders of magnitude.

What if my data are underdispersed?

The negative binomial cannot help, because Var = μ + μ²/φ is bounded below by μ. A binomial fits when there is a known denominator, such as tests performed. Otherwise the Conway-Maxwell-Poisson (Shmueli et al., 2005) covers both directions at the cost of an awkward normalising constant. Underdispersion in surveillance data more often points at rounding, capping or a reporting quota than at the biology.

Should the reporting delay go in the observation model or the transmission model?

Neither of the families here handles delay, because they change how much spread you allow and not what day an observation belongs to. A delay needs a distribution between the event and its report, which is a change to the structure of the model. The same holds for a reporting rate that changes over time: fitting a fixed ρ to data from a system whose testing capacity tripled will make the model infer a change in transmission that never happened.

Does the negative binomial fix the fit, or just the intervals?

On these data it does neither cleanly. It widens the intervals honestly, and it moves the posterior mean of R0 from 1.84 to 2.66, but the fitted trajectory still has one peak against an outbreak with two. Loosening the observation model buys permission to misfit. It does not buy a better mean.
