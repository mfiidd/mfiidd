Particle filters: propagate, weight, resample

A stochastic model does not give one trajectory per parameter set, so its likelihood is an average over every path the epidemic could have taken. A particle filter estimates that average by simulating paths, weighting them by the data, and keeping the ones the data support. This page runs the filter in front of you and shows what the estimate it returns is, and is not, good for.

The likelihood is an average over paths

One parameter set, one trajectory, if the model is deterministic

Fitting a deterministic model is easy in one specific way. A parameter set θ picks out exactly one trajectory, so the likelihood is one calculation: solve the equations, evaluate the observation model, stop.

A stochastic model has a latent path x1:T that θ does not determine. Two runs at the same θ take different paths, and we never see which path the real epidemic took. The likelihood of θ has to average over all of them, weighted by how probable each is under the model:

p(y1:T | θ) = Σx1:T p(y1:T | x1:T, θ) · p(x1:T | θ)

For a deterministic model the second factor is one for a single path and zero everywhere else, and the sum collapses to that one term. For a stochastic model nothing collapses. The count shown is the number of distinct sequences of daily case counts. The real path space is larger still, because it also holds the order of events within each day and the unobserved compartment states behind them.

Enumeration is hopeless, so we sample. Write the sum as an expectation over paths drawn from the model, and estimate it by simulating J of them:

p(y1:T | θ) = Ex ∼ model[ p(y1:T | x, θ) ]  ≈  1J Σj=1J p(y1:T | x(j), θ)

That estimate is unbiased and it is also useless. Each simulated path is scored against the whole series at once, so one path that happens to track the data collects almost all of the weight and the rest contribute nothing. The variance of the estimator grows exponentially with the number of observations.

The particle filter is this same estimator taken one observation at a time, with a resampling step in between that throws effort at the paths still in contention. For a model that forgets its own past, which epidemic models do, that single change makes the variance grow roughly linearly in T instead of exponentially.

Propagate, weight, resample

Step through the filter one observation at a time and watch the cloud of particles move

A particle is one simulated copy of the epidemic: a full state vector, carried forward in time. The filter keeps J of them and repeats three steps for each day of data.

  • Propagate. Run every particle forward one day under the model, from day t−1 to day t. This is a draw from p(xt | xt−1, θ), and it uses the model alone. The observation is not consulted, which is what makes this the bootstrap filter.
  • Weight. Score each particle by the probability it assigns to today's observation, wt(j) = p(yt | xt(j)). A particle predicting 20 cases when 21 were reported scores well. One predicting 60 scores close to zero.
  • Resample. Draw J particles with replacement in proportion to those weights. Particles with high weight are copied, particles with low weight disappear, and every survivor goes back to weight 1/J.

The running estimate of the likelihood is built from the weights as we go. Writing Wt−1(j) for the normalised weights carried into day t, the day's contribution is their weighted average of today's scores:

p̂(y1:T | θ) = ∏t=1T [ Σj=1J Wt−1(j) wt(j) ]     W0(j) = 1/J

After a resampling step every particle carries weight 1/J, so the bracket becomes the plain average (1/J) Σj wt(j) that the session quotes. That simpler form is only correct if you resample at every step. If you resample on a threshold instead, the carried weights are not equal and the general form above is the one to use.

The filter is identical either way, and only the propagate step changes.

The dispersion slider changes what the filter believes about reporting. The filter scores particles with a negative binomial of mean ρxt and dispersion k, whose variance is μ + μ2/k. At the top of the slider the filter uses the Poisson itself, which is the model the data were generated from. Lowering k tells the filter to expect more scatter than there really is, which flattens the weights and keeps more particles alive. Precise observations are harder for a bootstrap filter, because the model proposes without looking at yt and a sharp likelihood then rejects nearly everything it proposes.

Degeneracy and the effective sample size

Weights multiply, so without resampling one particle wins

Skip the resampling step and the weights compound. A particle's weight after t days is the product of its daily scores, so a particle that is slightly better on day 1 starts day 2 ahead, and the gap widens every day. Weight ratios are multiplicative and their spread grows without limit.

The standard diagnostic is the effective sample size, from Kong, Liu and Wong (1994). For normalised weights W(j):

ESS = 1Σj (W(j))2 = J1 + cv2(w)     1 ≤ ESS ≤ J

ESS equals J when the weights are equal and drops to 1 when a single particle holds all of the weight. The second form says what it is measuring: cv is the coefficient of variation of the unnormalised weights, so ESS falls as soon as the weights become uneven. It is a rough diagnostic rather than a real sample size, and it is blind to a particle set that agrees with itself while being wrong.

The red line is plain importance sampling from the model, which is what the filter reduces to when resampling is switched off. The three log-likelihood values in the chips come from one run each, so their ordering means nothing. All three are unbiased estimates of the same quantity and they differ in variance, which takes many runs to see.

Resampling at every step is not free. When the weights are already near-uniform it copies particles for no reason, which adds variance and destroys diversity. Resampling only when ESS falls below J/2 is the usual compromise, and the threshold is a convention rather than a result.

The estimate is unbiased, and its logarithm is not

The distinction the next session depends on

The filter returns a number, and the sampler in the next session consumes it. What it returns is

log p̂(y1:T | θ) = Σt=1T log [ Σj=1J Wt−1(j) wt(j) ]

The property that matters is exact and it holds for any number of particles, including J = 1:

E[ p̂(y1:T | θ) ] = p(y1:T | θ)

The expectation is over the filter's own randomness at fixed θ and fixed data. This is the result in Del Moral (2004, Proposition 7.4.1), and it is the reason particle marginal Metropolis-Hastings targets the right posterior (Andrieu, Doucet and Holenstein 2010). More particles do not remove a bias in the likelihood estimate, because there is none. They reduce its variance.

The logarithm is a different matter. Jensen's inequality applies to the concave function log, so

E[ log p̂(y | θ) ] ≤ log E[ p̂(y | θ) ] = log p(y | θ)

The log-likelihood estimate is biased low, always, and by more when the estimator is noisier. Under the Gaussian approximation to the error, log p̂ is centred at log p − σ2/2 with standard deviation σ, which is exactly the offset that keeps p̂ itself unbiased. Bérard, Del Moral and Doucet (2014) prove this limit under mixing conditions, with T and J growing together.

The figure runs the filter 60 times at the same θ and shows both quantities. The mean of the log estimates climbs towards the truth as J grows. The log of the mean of the estimates barely moves, because the mean of the estimates is unbiased for the likelihood at any particle count.

Sixty runs at each particle count, all at the same parameters and the same data, differing only in the filter's random draws. A run "dies" when every particle is given weight zero on some day, which returns minus infinity. Those runs are left out of the mean and the standard deviation of the logs, and kept in the mean on the natural scale, where a dead run is an estimate of zero like any other. The log of the mean is itself estimated from those sixty runs, so it wanders a little at small J, where a few runs carry most of the mass.

Two things to read off. The spread shrinks roughly as 1/√J, so halving the standard deviation costs four times the computation. The gap between the two vertical lines tracks SD2/2 once the estimator is not too noisy, which is the Jensen bias made concrete.

In this small example a standard deviation of 1 to 3 is reached somewhere around J = 8 to 16. The SEIT4L model in the practical needs closer to 64, because the variance grows with the number of observations and with how tightly the model has to track them.

Where the 1 to 3 target comes from, and what it costs the sampler to sit outside it, is worked through on the particle MCMC page.

Path degeneracy: a good present, a poor past

Trace the surviving particles backwards and they collapse onto one ancestor

Resampling fixes the weights and creates a second problem in their place. Every copy made at day t shares its entire history before t with its parent. Lineages die and are never recreated, so the number of distinct ancestors of the current particle set falls monotonically as you look further back, and eventually reaches one. How far back that takes depends on J. At J = 10 the collapse is complete well inside these 25 days. At J = 80 the count is still above one on day 1, and it is already down to a small fraction of J.

Raising J pushes the collapse further back but does not prevent it. The whole ancestry is cheap to keep for the same reason: Jacob, Murray and Rubenthaler (2015) show the tree has expected size of order J log J nodes under mixing conditions, because almost all of it is a single trunk.

The consequence is specific. The filter gives a decent sample of the state now, at the final observation, because those particles were resampled recently and are still distinct. It gives a bad sample of the state early on, because all the surviving particles inherited that part of the path from one ancestor. Anything you compute from the early part of the stored trajectories has an effective sample size close to one, whatever the ESS chip said at the time.

This is why you do not draw many latent paths from one filter run. In particle MCMC the filter is re-run at every iteration, and a single lineage is drawn from each run. The posterior over paths is built up across iterations of the sampler, and each contributing path comes from an independent filter.

Smoothing algorithms exist for the cases where the early path is the target. Fixed-lag smoothing accepts a small bias for a large variance reduction, backward simulation resamples the path after the forward pass, and ancestor sampling repairs the same problem inside particle Gibbs (Lindsten, Jordan and Schön 2014). None of them is needed to fit parameters, which is why the course does not reach for them.

Common questions

The ones that come up in the practical

Why does resampling not throw information away?

The particles it deletes had negligible weight, so they were contributing almost nothing to any average. Their contribution to the likelihood has already been banked in the running product before they are deleted. What resampling costs is diversity in the stored paths, which is the subject of the path degeneracy section.

Why is my log-likelihood estimate different every run?

The estimate is a Monte Carlo average over the particles, and the particles are random. Nothing about the data or the parameters has changed. Set the seed to reproduce a run, and increase J to reduce the spread.

How many particles do I need?

Enough that the standard deviation of the log-likelihood estimate is around 1 to 3 at a parameter set near the posterior mode. Measure it by running the filter 100 or more times at fixed parameters and taking the standard deviation of the results. The number that works at one parameter set will be too small elsewhere, so leave headroom.

Can I average several log-likelihood estimates to reduce the noise?

Averaging the logs keeps the downward bias of a single run while hiding it behind a tighter spread. Average the estimates on the natural scale instead. That average is still unbiased for the likelihood, which is the property particle MCMC needs, and its logarithm carries the same low bias in smaller measure. Averaging R runs costs the same as one filter with R times as many particles, and the single larger filter is the better estimator, so spend the budget there.

Is the bootstrap filter the only option?

No. It proposes from the model alone, which is simple and needs nothing beyond a simulator. Guided and auxiliary filters propose using the current observation, so their particles are aimed at the data before they are weighted. They help most when the observations are precise, which is exactly when the bootstrap filter wastes particles.

Why does the filter degenerate faster with more data?

Each observation multiplies the weights again, so the variance of the log-likelihood estimate grows roughly linearly with the number of observations. A particle count calibrated on 30 days will not hold up on 300 days at the same standard deviation.

Does resampling at every step hurt?

It adds variance whenever the weights are already close to uniform, since it copies and deletes particles for no gain. It also accelerates path degeneracy. Resampling when ESS falls below J/2 avoids both, and the likelihood estimate stays unbiased under either policy.

My filter returned minus infinity. What happened?

Every particle was given weight zero on some day, which happens when no simulated particle can produce that day's observation at all. A Poisson observation model with a particle predicting zero cases and a positive count observed does exactly this. More particles, a looser observation model, or a model that can reach the data will each fix it, and only the last of those fixes the underlying problem.
