Stochastic epidemic models

On an island of 284 people, transmission is a sequence of individual chance events rather than a smooth flow. This page runs the Gillespie algorithm one event at a time, measures how far the deterministic solution sits from the average stochastic run, and shows why the SEIT4L model splits temporary immunity into four compartments.

The model behind every figure on this page

SEITL on Tristan da Cunha, written as five transitions between whole individuals

In August 1971 a ship landed five islanders on Tristan da Cunha. Influenza A/H3N2 spread through the village, subsided after three weeks, and then came back. Of the 284 islanders, 273 had at least one episode of illness and 92 had two. The dataset dates 312 of the 365 recorded attacks to within a day, over 59 days (Mantle and Tyrrell 1973).

The SEITL model explains the second wave with a temporary immune stage T. A fraction α of those leaving T acquire long-term immunity L, and the rest return to S and can be infected again (Camacho et al. 2011). The deterministic version is five ordinary differential equations. The stochastic version is the same model read as a continuous-time Markov chain, in which the right-hand sides become rates at which individual events happen.

TransitionRateEffect on the state
S → Eβ S I / N,  β = R0/DinfS − 1, E + 1
E → Iε E,  ε = 1/DlatE − 1, I + 1, one new case
I → Tν I,  ν = 1/DinfI − 1, T + 1
T → S(1 − α) τ T,  τ = 1/DimmT − 1, S + 1
T → Lα τ TT − 1, L + 1

The two versions are the same set of rates used in two ways. The deterministic model treats them as continuous flows, so S can take the value 0.37. The stochastic model treats them as the intensities of a jump process, so S is always a whole number and the epidemic can hit zero infectious individuals and stop. Everything on this page follows from that difference.

Unless a control says otherwise the figures use the parameters the course session settles on: R0 = 10, Dlat = 2 days, Dinf = 2 days, α = 0.5, Dimm = 13 days. The initial state is S = 279, I = 2, T = 3, giving N = 284: three of the five returning islanders had been ill during the eight day voyage and are past their infectious period, and two fell ill on landing. The first three figures use the single-stage SEITL. The number of T stages becomes a control in the Erlang section.

Every number on this page is computed in your browser as you read it. The random draws come from a seeded generator, so a figure looks the same each time you load it, and the buttons labelled new seed give you a different run of the same model.

The Gillespie algorithm, one event at a time

Rates in, a waiting time and an event out

The direct method (Gillespie 1976, 1977) simulates the Markov chain by answering two questions at each step: how long until something happens, and what happens. Both answers come from the rate vector.

Write aj for the rate of transition j at the current state and a0 = Σj aj for their sum. Because every transition is a competing exponential clock, the time until the first of them fires is exponential with rate a0, and the one that fires is transition j with probability aj/a0. Two uniform draws u1, u2 give both:

a0 = Σj aj      τ = −ln u1a0      fire event k where  Σj<k aj < u2 a0 ≤ Σj≤k aj

Then advance the clock by τ, apply the state change, and recompute the rates. Nothing is discretised and no time step is chosen, which is what makes the method exact for this model.

Press run to day 6 and read the bottom strip. With R0 = 10 the total rate starts at 11.05 per day, passes 150 per day around day 2 to 3, and is back near 90 by day 6, so the mean waiting time falls by more than a factor of ten and then recovers. Every event costs the same work whatever the rate, which is why the steep part of an epidemic is where the simulation time goes. Press run to the end and the whole shape appears: about 1900 events for one 59 day trajectory on this island.
What the algorithm assumes. The rates depend on the current state alone and stay constant between events, which is what allows the exponential waiting time. That is exactly the memoryless assumption the Erlang section takes apart. Splitting a compartment into stages keeps the algorithm intact and changes only the number of rates.

The deterministic trajectory is not the mean of the stochastic ones

Averaging runs that peak on different days flattens the peak

It is tempting to read the ODE solution as the average behaviour and the stochastic runs as noise around it. The algebra says otherwise. Take the expected number of susceptibles in the Markov chain:

d E[S] / dt = − βN E[S I] + (1−α) τ E[T]   and   E[S I] = E[S] E[I] + Cov(S, I)

The deterministic equations are what you get by dropping that covariance. It is not zero. A run that is ahead of the others has both more infectious individuals and fewer susceptibles left, so Cov(S, I) turns negative once depletion starts, and the mean of the process does not satisfy the ODE. What you see on a plot is the timing version of the same fact. Runs peak on different days, so averaging them at fixed times spreads one peak over several days and lowers it.

Kurtz (1970, 1971) gives the condition under which the two do agree. Scaled by N, the Markov chain converges to the ODE solution over any finite time window as N grows, provided the initial state also scales with N. That proviso is doing real work, and the seeding control below turns it off so you can watch the convergence fail.

At N = 284 the mean peak is about 12% below the ODE peak and a day late. Drag N up with seeding scaled with N and the two curves close on each other, which is Kurtz's limit arriving. Now press the last two presets in turn. At R0 = 3 with a scaled seed the ratio reaches about 0.98 by N = 3000; with the seed fixed at 2 infectious it stalls near 0.75 and the spread of peak days stays at three to four days however large N is. The epidemic still starts from two individuals, so the random delay before it takes off never shrinks. The chip for runs that took off also stays short of 100%, and the pointwise mean includes the runs that never started. Both the fizzles and the delay survive as N grows.
The practical consequence. Fitting a deterministic model and treating the residuals as observation noise assumes the deterministic curve is the centre of the stochastic distribution. On an island of 284 it is not, so the fit absorbs the mismatch into the parameters. This is the reason the course moves to particle filters rather than fitting the ODE and widening the error bars.

Demographic noise scales as 1 / √N

Measured on this model, including where the law does not yet apply

Kurtz's central limit theorem (1971) says the fluctuation of the counts about the deterministic path is of order √N, so relative to the population it is of order 1/√N. The same result comes out of van Kampen's system-size expansion. Both describe the leading term as N grows. Neither says how large N has to be before that term dominates, and on this model the answer turns out to matter.

The figure measures it. For each population size it runs the model many times, discards the runs where the epidemic never took off, and computes the coefficient of variation of the outcome across the rest. On log axes a 1/√N law is a straight line of slope −1/2. The dashed line is that law anchored at the largest population shown, and two fitted slopes are reported: one over the whole range, one over the upper half.

The seed is held at I0 infectious individuals at every population size rather than scaled with it, which matches the island and makes the lower strip readable.

A run counts as having taken off if its total incidence exceeds N/10. The coefficient of variation is computed over those runs only, because a mixture of major outbreaks and immediate failures has a large CV for a reason that has nothing to do with the √N scaling. Raise the replicate count if the points look ragged.

Read the upper half of the axis first. Above about N = 500 the points sit on the dashed line and the fitted slope is close to −1/2, which is the law working. Below that they lift away from it, and at N = 284 the final size is noisier than the law would predict. An asymptotic result describes the direction of travel, and using it as a formula at the island's size overstates how quiet the model is there. Switch the outcome to peak height and the small-N points fall a little below the line instead. Which way a particular summary departs is not something the asymptotic result tells you.

Failing to start is set by the seed, not by the population size

The lower strip is the fraction of runs in which the epidemic never took off. Read it from left to right: it does not trend with N. That contradicts the natural guess that small populations are the ones that fizzle.

Approximate the start of the epidemic by a branching process, in which each infectious individual infects a fresh person at rate β and recovers at rate ν. The number it infects is then geometric with mean R0, the extinction probability of a single line of descent solves s = 1/(1 + R0 − R0 s), which gives s = 1/R0 for R0 > 1, and I0 independent lines all have to die out:

P(fade out) ≈ 1R0 I0      R0 = 10, I0 = 2  →  1%

N does not appear. The approximation holds while susceptible depletion is negligible, which is the phase that decides whether the epidemic starts at all, so the prediction sits close to the measured bars across the whole axis. Drag I0 and R0 and the bars follow the line. At R0 = 10 the predicted 1% takes many replicates to measure, so set R0 to 3 or 4 to see the agreement at once. See Whittle (1955) for the original argument and Andersson and Britton (2000) for the modern treatment.

What N does control. Not whether the epidemic starts, but how far a run that starts wanders from the deterministic path, and whether it survives the trough between the two waves. At N = 284 the trough holds a handful of infectious individuals, and a handful can reach zero by chance. That is an extinction risk that does scale with N, and it is why a second wave on this island is an uncertain event rather than a foregone conclusion.

Exponential or Erlang waiting times

Why the model in the practical is called SEIT4L

One compartment with a constant exit rate τ gives an exponential dwell time with mean D = 1/τ. Its density is largest at zero and falls away from there, so the single most likely outcome for someone who has just entered temporary immunity is to leave immediately. Applied to immunity that is a strange claim, and it is a consequence of the compartment structure rather than of anything biological.

Chain k compartments, each with exit rate k/D. The total time is a sum of k exponential waiting times, which is Erlang with shape k and the same mean D:

f(x) = (k/D)k xk−1 e−kx/D(k − 1)!      mean = D    SD = D√k    CV = 1√k    mode = D k − 1k

The mean is fixed as k changes, so nothing about the average duration of immunity is being altered. What changes is the spread and the position of the mode. At k = 1 the mode is at zero. At k = 4 it is at three quarters of the mean, and the coefficient of variation has halved. Wearing, Rohani and Keeling (2005) and Lloyd (2001) work through what this does to epidemic dynamics in general.

The lower panel is the consequence. Susceptibles are handed back to S by the exit from the last T stage, and the shape of that exit is the shape of the second wave. With k = 1 they trickle back from the moment the first person enters T, so reinfection is spread thinly over weeks. With k = 4 they return as a cohort, and the second wave stands clear of the tail of the first.

At k = 1 there is no second peak at all with these parameters, only a long flat shoulder, because susceptibles start returning to S the moment the first person enters T. The readout says none. Slide k up and a peak appears on day 28 and grows away from the trough: about 1.4 times the trough at k = 2, 2.6 at k = 4, and 6.2 at k = 10. Meanwhile the CV of the dwell time falls as 1/√k and the mode moves out from zero towards the mean. The date of the second peak barely moves, so what k controls is how sharply the wave stands out rather than when it happens.
Why four. Four is a compromise rather than a measurement. It buys most of the change in shape, since the CV falls from 1 to 0.5 between k = 1 and k = 4 but only to 0.41 by k = 6, and it costs three extra state variables. Camacho et al. (2011) compared variants of the model on these data and preferred the four-stage version.

When the deterministic model is good enough

The number that decides is not N

Stochastic simulation is expensive and stochastic inference is much more expensive, so the honest question is when you can avoid both. The rough condition is that the smallest number of individuals in the compartments driving the dynamics stays large throughout the period you care about.

That is a statement about the trajectory rather than about the population. Fluctuations in a compartment holding m individuals are of order √m, so they matter relatively when √m is a noticeable fraction of m, which is to say when m is small. A city of ten million with four infectious people is in the stochastic regime, and an island of 284 in the middle of a wave with sixty infectious people is close to the deterministic one. The places to check are therefore the beginning, the end, and any trough in between.

  • Take-off and fade-out are always stochastic. No population size fixes this. If the question is whether an outbreak happens, or when it will be over, the deterministic model cannot answer it, because it never reaches zero.
  • Troughs are stochastic. A multi-wave epidemic passes through a small number of infectious individuals between waves. The Tristan da Cunha trough is where the SEITL model is least deterministic.
  • The middle of a large wave is close to deterministic. Relative fluctuation of order 1/√N there once N is well above the island's size, and invisible at N = 106.
  • Observation noise is a separate question. A deterministic trajectory with a Poisson observation model is a perfectly reasonable description of a large outbreak, and it is much cheaper to fit. It is wrong here because the process itself is noisy, not because the counts are. The observation models page takes that question up on the same 59 days, fitting a single-wave SIR at R0 near 2 whose misfit is structural.
The middle ground. Between exact simulation and the ODE sit tau-leaping (Gillespie 2001), which fires a Poisson number of each event over a short interval, and the chemical Langevin or diffusion approximation, which replaces the jumps with a continuous process whose noise term carries the √N scaling. Both are cheaper than the direct method and both fail in exactly the regime this section says to worry about, where compartments hold few individuals.

Common questions

The ones that come up in the practical

Why not just add noise to the ODE solution?

Because that is observation noise, and the problem here is process noise. Adding noise to the ODE keeps the deterministic curve as the centre of the distribution, and the second section shows the true mean is somewhere else. It also cannot produce extinction or a run that peaks four days late.

The principled version of the idea is the diffusion approximation, which adds a noise term inside the differential equation rather than to its solution. That is a real approximation to the Markov chain and it is accurate when compartments are large.

Why is my stochastic run different from my neighbour's?

Because the model is a distribution over trajectories rather than a single trajectory, and each run is one draw from it. Identical parameters give different output by construction.

Setting a seed makes your own run reproducible. It does not make it typical, so never draw a conclusion from one run.

Is the Gillespie algorithm an approximation?

No. Given the rates, the direct method draws exactly from the law of the continuous-time Markov chain. There is no time step and no discretisation error, and running it with a smaller anything will not change the answer.

Tau-leaping is the approximate relative: it holds the rates fixed over a short interval and fires a Poisson number of each event, which is fast and wrong when a compartment can empty within the interval.

How many replicates do I need?

Enough that the quantity you are reading has settled. The standard error of a mean over m runs falls as 1/√m, so going from 100 to 400 runs halves it. A few hundred is usually plenty for a mean or a median, and a few thousand for a tail probability such as the chance of no second wave.

The test is to run the whole thing again with a different seed and see whether your conclusion changes.

Why does an epidemic with R0 above 1 sometimes fail to start?

Because R0 is an average and the first few infectious individuals may recover before infecting anyone. With one initial case the chance of that is about 1/R0, so even R0 = 10 fails one time in ten.

The Tristan da Cunha seed is two infectious islanders, giving roughly 1%. The deterministic model gives 0%, which is the one number it can never get right.

Why four T compartments and not two or twenty?

The returns diminish quickly. The coefficient of variation of the dwell time is 1/√k, so going from one stage to four halves it, and going from four to sixteen only halves it again at the cost of twelve more state variables.

Four gives a clearly non-exponential shape and keeps the model small enough to fit with a particle filter. Twenty would be close to a fixed delay, which the data on 284 people cannot distinguish from four.

Why is fitting a stochastic model so much harder?

Because the likelihood of the data given the parameters is an integral over every possible trajectory, and there is no formula for it. The deterministic model has one trajectory per parameter set, so its likelihood is a direct calculation.

A particle filter estimates that integral by simulating many trajectories and reweighting them against the data. That estimate is unbiased, which is what lets it stand in for the true likelihood inside MCMC.

Does the stochastic model have a basic reproduction number?

Yes, and it is the same R0 = β/ν, the mean number of infections caused by one infectious individual in a wholly susceptible population. What changes is that the realised number is a random variable, geometrically distributed for exponential infectious periods.

That variability is the whole reason an epidemic with R0 = 10 can still fail.
