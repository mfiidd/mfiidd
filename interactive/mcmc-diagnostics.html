MCMC diagnostics: R̂, ESS and MCSE

Every number on this page comes from one chain generator that you control. Turn up the autocorrelation, start the chains in the wrong place, or let them drift, and watch which diagnostic notices and which one does not. The companion page on NUTS covers the sampler these diagnostics are usually run on.

A chain you can break

One generator, four diagnostics, all recomputed as you drag

The practical fitted an SIR model and read R̂ and ESS off a summary table. Here the chains come from an AR(1) process instead, so the thing being diagnosed is under your control. Each draw is a weighted average of the previous draw and fresh noise:

xt = μ + ρ (xt−1 − μ) + σ√1 − ρ²  zt,    zt ~ Normal(0, 1)

The target is Normal(μ = 2.5, σ = 0.25), standing in for the posterior of R0. The square root on the noise holds the marginal standard deviation at σ whatever ρ is, so the distribution the chains explore never changes. Only the speed at which they explore it does. When ESS falls it falls because the draws repeat each other, not because the target got wider.

One chain gives the diagnostics nothing to compare. A single trace can settle into a steady band and stay there for the whole run while sitting in the wrong place, or in one mode of a posterior that has two. Every number computed from those draws will then agree with every other. Set the chain count below to one and the between-chain comparison has nothing to work with, because there are no chain means to take a variance of, so R̂ as the next section defines it cannot be formed. Only the split version survives, and it does not care where the chain is. It reads 1.000 with the separation slider at zero and 0.999 with it at the maximum, which has moved the chain's target more than a posterior standard deviation. Chains started from dispersed points are what give a chain in the wrong place something to disagree with.

Convergence itself is asymptotic. The chain's distribution approaches the target as the run grows without bound, and no finite run can show that it has arrived. R̂ and ESS can only fail to find evidence against convergence, so R̂ = 1.00 is consistent with a chain that has not converged. What follows is a set of ways to be caught out. Passing all of them is not a proof.

R̂ compares two estimates of the same variance

W within, B between, and the ratio that only equals one when the chains agree

Gelman and Rubin (1992) built the statistic from two variance estimates. Take m chains of length n. Compute the variance inside each chain and average those, giving W. Compute the variance of the m chain means and multiply by n, giving B. Both estimate the posterior variance, and while the chains are still spread out they fail in opposite directions. W only sees where each chain has already been, so it comes out too small. The pooled estimate V̂ mixes the two and comes out too large, because B is inflated by the disagreement:

W = 1m Σj sj²      B = nm−1 Σj (x̄j − x̄)²
V̂ = n−1n W + 1n B      R̂ = √ V̂W 

B is n times a variance, so its size is not comparable with W directly. The comparison happens through B/n. In the figure each chain carries a band of ± its own standard deviation, and W is the average of those squared half-widths. The bracket on the right spans ± the standard deviation of the chain means, which is √(B/n). Once the chains have converged, a chain mean varies by about √(Wτ/n), where τ is the integrated autocorrelation time defined in the ESS section. So B comes out at about τ times W, and R̂ approaches 1 because B/n stays far smaller than W. The general form is R̂ = √(1 + (τ−1)/n), which is 1.003 at the default settings, where τ is about 4. Drag the chains apart and the bracket grows while the bands do not.

What a given R̂ is worth in the units you care about. Since B/n is the variance of the chain means and V̂ ≈ W + B/n, the ratio is close to R̂² ≈ 1 + var(x̄j)/W. Invert it. R̂ = 1.01 means the chain means are spread by about 0.14 posterior standard deviations, and R̂ = 1.1 means about 0.46 of one. Drag the separation slider and read the two numbers together to check. That is the argument for the 1.01 threshold recommended by Vehtari, Gelman, Simpson, Carpenter and Bürkner (2021). A run passing at 1.1 has chains disagreeing about the centre by half a posterior standard deviation, which is enough to change what you would write down.

The value reported by rhat() in MCMCChains.jl differs from the definition above in two ways. It splits each chain in half first, which the next section covers. It also rank normalises the draws, replacing each value by the normal quantile of its rank among all the draws pooled, and takes the larger of R̂ on the rank normalised draws and R̂ on the folded draws |x − median|. Rank normalising makes R̂ defined even when the posterior has no finite variance, and folding lets it catch chains that share a centre but differ in scale.

Splitting each chain in half

The version that catches a chain still going somewhere

R̂ as defined above asks whether the chains agree with each other. It has nothing to say about a chain that disagrees with itself. Here every chain starts in the same place and drifts the same way, which is what a run that has not finished its warmup looks like. The chain means stay close together, so B stays small and R̂ sits at 1.00. Split each chain into a first half and a second half, treat the halves as separate chains, and the drift becomes a disagreement between chains.

Splitting is also what makes R̂ usable at all on a single chain. With one chain there is no between-chain variance to compute, so the definition in the previous section returns nothing. Two halves of one chain give it something to compare.

The two halves of each chain are shown with their own mean. The full-chain mean, which is what the unsplit R̂ compares, is the average of the two.

A drift of one posterior standard deviation across the run leaves the unsplit R̂ where it was, at 1.003, and pushes split-R̂ past 1.01. Watch the pooled mean at the same time. It moves with the drift, so the diagnostic that passes is passing a chain whose answer is wrong.

The unsplit value can settle slightly below 1. V̂ is a weighted average of W and B, so chains whose means agree more closely than their own spread implies give V̂ < W. Reading 0.999 as reassurance is a mistake. It means the between-chain comparison has found nothing, which is exactly what a shared drift produces.

ESS comes out of the autocorrelation

One sum, truncated where the estimated correlations become noise

The variance of the mean of N independent draws is σ²/N. For a correlated chain it is σ²τ/N, where τ is the integrated autocorrelation time. The effective sample size is the number of independent draws that would have given the same variance:

ESS = Nτ,     τ = 1 + 2 Σk=1 K ρk

The upper limit K is where the honest version differs from the one usually quoted. Written as an infinite sum this does not work. Each ρk is estimated from the same finite chain, so at large lags the estimates are noise around zero, and adding thousands of them adds noise rather than signal. Geyer (1992) supplies the fix used by Stan and by MCMCChains.jl. Add the autocorrelations in consecutive pairs, ρ2k + ρ2k+1, and stop at the first pair whose sum goes negative. For a reversible chain those pair sums are positive and decreasing in theory, so the first negative one is the point at which the estimate has stopped tracking the truth. The sequence is then forced to be decreasing before the sum is taken.

The bars are the autocorrelations that the estimator actually uses, computed from the split chains and pooled across them. Bars past the truncation lag are drawn in grey and contribute nothing. The dashed line is ρk, the exact autocorrelation of the AR(1) process the chains came from, so you can see how much of the wobble in the bars is estimation error.

Why ESS can exceed the number of draws

Set ρ negative. The chain now overshoots on every step, so a draw above the mean is usually followed by one below it. The odd autocorrelations are negative, τ drops below 1, and ESS comes out larger than the number of draws. At ρ = −0.5 the sum gives τ = 1/3 and about three times as many effective draws as actual ones. Nothing has gone wrong. The chain is averaging out its own error faster than independent draws would, which is what the session refers to when it says NUTS sometimes produces mildly anti-correlated draws. NUTS produces them because the trajectory it builds moves away from the current point before a draw is taken from it, so consecutive draws sit on opposite sides of the typical set more often than chance would give (Hoffman and Gelman 2014). The NUTS page builds that trajectory step by step.

The reported value is capped at S log10S for S total draws, because the estimator itself becomes unreliable when τ is very small. Push ρ to −0.8 and the cap binds, so the number stops rising even though the theoretical ESS keeps going.

Bulk and tail

Bulk ESS applies everything above to the rank normalised draws, so it describes how well the centre of the distribution is estimated. Tail ESS is the smaller of the effective sample sizes for the 5% and 95% quantile indicators, which is what the endpoint of a credible interval depends on. Check it before quoting an interval. On this page, while ρ is positive, tail ESS comes out larger than bulk ESS, because an indicator of a Gaussian AR(1) crossing a fixed threshold decorrelates faster than the draws themselves do. Click antithetic and the order reverses, since the negative correlation is what bulk ESS picks up. It also reverses for the posteriors that motivate the statistic, where a heavy tail or a funnel is visited rarely and left slowly. Vehtari et al. (2021) recommend at least 100 effective draws per chain, so 400 with the usual four chains, before either the ESS or the R̂ estimate is worth trusting.

MCSE is the number you report against

How many of the digits in your posterior mean are yours to keep

ESS on its own does not say whether a run was long enough. It becomes actionable through the Monte Carlo standard error, the standard deviation of the posterior mean estimate over hypothetical repeat runs of the same sampler:

MCSE ≈ posterior SD√ESS

A digit at the d-th decimal place is worth writing down when MCSE is smaller than half of 10−d, because a rerun would then leave that digit alone. The spread of the reruns is the standard deviation of the dots. It should sit close to the MCSE once there are a handful of them, because that is what MCSE predicts.

MCSE turns "run it longer" into a stopping rule. Halving it costs four times the draws, since ESS grows in proportion to the run length and MCSE falls with its square root. Decide the precision you need first. Quoting R0 = 2.31 when the MCSE is 0.05 claims a second decimal place that belongs to the random number generator.

MCSE measures noise and says nothing about bias. Set the starting point in the first figure far from the target and the dots keep the same spread while sitting to one side of the target mean. A tight MCSE around the wrong number is still the wrong number.

What the diagnostics cannot see

They report on the sampler, and only on the part of the target it reached

R̂ and ESS compare draws with other draws. Both are answers about the sampler, and about the region of the target the sampler visited. If every chain starts in the same mode of a bimodal posterior and none of them crosses, they agree with each other and with themselves, so R̂ stays at 1.00 and ESS stays high while half the posterior is missing. Dispersed starting points and more chains raise the chance of noticing, and they do not turn it into a guarantee.

Neither number knows anything about whether the model is the right model for the data. A converged, well-mixed fit of a model that omits the mechanism that generated your epidemic gives clean diagnostics and wrong answers. That question belongs to the posterior predictive checks in the model checking session.

Common questions

The ones worth asking after the practical

How many chains should I run?

Four is the usual default and the smallest number that makes R̂ informative in practice. More chains give the between-chain variance more to work with, and starting them from dispersed points is what lets R̂ detect a warmup that has not finished.

Is thinning ever worth it?

Not for estimating a posterior mean. Discarding draws throws away information and raises the Monte Carlo error slightly, even when the draws are highly correlated. Thin when storage or the cost of whatever you do with each draw is the binding constraint, and accept the small loss.

Should I use bulk ESS or tail ESS?

Both. Bulk ESS covers the centre and governs the MCSE of the mean, and tail ESS covers the 5% and 95% quantiles and governs the endpoints of a credible interval. On a posterior with a heavy tail or a funnel, tail ESS is the smaller of the two, so the interval is less well estimated than the mean it surrounds.

My R̂ is fine and the trace still looks bad. What now?

Trust the trace. Chains that share a starting region and drift together pass the between-chain comparison, which is the case split-R̂ exists to catch. Check split-R̂, check ESS per chain, and disperse the starting points before rerunning.

ESS is low. Should I just run for longer?

Running longer raises ESS in proportion to the extra draws, so it fixes the noise. It does not fix the geometry that caused τ to be large. If τ is in the hundreds, reparametrising or changing sampler buys far more than another zero on the iteration count.
