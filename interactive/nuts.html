NUTS: sliding downhill, and knowing when to stop

The practical calls Hamiltonian Monte Carlo a puck sliding along the posterior surface. That picture hides the three things that actually matter: the momentum you have to invent, the energy error the integrator makes, and the U-turn rule that ends each trajectory. Every figure here runs the real algorithm in your browser. The companion page on MCMC diagnostics defines R̂, ESS and MCSE.

The proposal is a physical trajectory

Invent a momentum, slide for a while, and propose where you land

Metropolis-Hastings proposes a point. Hamiltonian Monte Carlo proposes the endpoint of a trajectory, and the trajectory is generated by the posterior itself.

Write the negative log-posterior as a potential energy, U(θ) = −log p(θ | y). Now add a momentum vector p, one component per parameter, drawn fresh at the start of every iteration from p ~ N(0, M). The matrix M is the mass matrix, and it is ours to choose. Together θ and p have a total energy:

H(θ, p) = U(θ) + ½ p⁠T M−1 p    // potential + kinetic

The joint density exp(−H) factorises into the posterior for θ and a Gaussian for p. So if we can move around at constant H and then throw the momentum away, we are left with a draw from the posterior. Hamilton's equations describe exactly that motion. Solving them exactly is not possible for a real posterior, so we integrate them numerically with the leapfrog scheme:

p ← p − (ε/2) ∇U(θ)   // half-kickθ ← θ + ε M−1 p       // driftp ← p − (ε/2) ∇U(θ)   // half-kick

The gradient enters only through the kicks. This is where the differentiable model earns its keep: the ODE solver propagates derivatives, so ∇U is available at roughly the cost of U itself.

The teal arrow is εM−1p, so its tip is where the drift would put θ. A kick moves that tip by the amber vector and leaves θ where it is. The half-kicks change p and leave θ alone. The drift changes θ and leaves p alone. Splitting the momentum update in two is what makes the scheme reversible and volume preserving, which is why a plain Metropolis correction on the endpoint is valid.

The Metropolis correction at the end uses the energy error and nothing else. If the integrator were exact, ΔH would be zero and every proposal would be accepted, however far away it landed. That is the whole point of using dynamics. The acceptance probability does not decay with distance. It decays with integration error.
accept with probability  min(1, e−ΔH),  ΔH = H(θnew, pnew) − H(θold, pold)

Switch the mass matrix to dense and the ridge disappears from the sampler's point of view. Setting M = Σ−1 makes the equation of motion θ̈ = −M−1Σ−1θ isotropic, so every direction oscillates at the same rate and one trajectory sweeps the whole posterior. This is what warmup is trying to estimate. Turing and Stan both default to a diagonal version, which fixes differing scales but leaves correlation alone.

Step size, energy error and divergence

The honest definition of a divergent transition

The leapfrog scheme does not conserve H exactly. For a well-chosen ε the error stays bounded and oscillates. Past a threshold the error grows geometrically with every step and the trajectory flies off. For a Gaussian target the threshold is exact, and it explains the rest of this page.

A Gaussian target with covariance Σ and mass matrix M gives linear dynamics with oscillation frequencies ωi equal to the square roots of the eigenvalues of M−1Σ−1. Leapfrog is stable for a harmonic oscillator only while εω < 2, so the trajectory holds together only while

ε < εmax = 2ωmax    // with M = I this is twice the smallest posterior scale

The narrowest direction of the posterior sets the step size for the whole sampler. Drag ε past the limit reported in the readout and the energy error goes from a flat wobble to a straight line on the log axis.

What a divergence is. Both Stan and AdvancedHMC.jl, which is what Turing runs, flag a divergent transition when the energy error along a trajectory exceeds a fixed threshold. In Turing this is the Δ_max keyword to NUTS, defaulting to 1000, which is handed to AdvancedHMC.jl. Nothing subtler is going on. A divergence is the sampler noticing that its integrator has broken down, and it reports each one so you can see where.

Turing records the flag per iteration in the numerical_error column of the chain, which is the column the diagnostics session sums to count divergences. A divergence matters because the sampler stops the trajectory there and stays put. The states it fails to reach are exactly the states that broke the integrator, so those regions are systematically under-sampled. Divergences bias the posterior in a direction you cannot see from the trace plot.

The U-turn rule

Why the trajectory length cannot simply be fixed, and why it cannot simply be greedy either

A trajectory that is too short wastes gradients and behaves like a random walk. A trajectory that is too long is worse than wasteful: the puck comes back round and the proposal lands near where it started. The right length depends on where in the posterior you are, so no single number works.

Hoffman and Gelman (2014) turned this into a stopping rule. Track the two ends of the trajectory, θ− and θ+, with their momenta. The trajectory has started to double back when the vector joining the ends stops agreeing with the velocity at either end:

(θ+ − θ−) · M−1p− < 0    or    (θ+ − θ−) · M−1p+ < 0    // stop

Halve ε and it takes about one more doubling to cover the same path. The path the sampler travels is set by the posterior, and the step size only sets how finely that path is chopped up. At the left end of the slider, below about ε = 0.05, the cap of 7 doublings binds instead and the count stops rising while the number of states keeps growing. The readout also says which check stopped it: often a subtree turns before the two outer ends do, and that is enough to stop the doubling.
Why doubling, and why in both directions. A greedy rule that simply integrated forwards and stopped at the first U-turn would be wrong. The stopping point would depend on where the trajectory started, so the reverse move would not be available with the same probability and detailed balance would fail. NUTS avoids this by building a balanced binary tree: it repeatedly doubles the trajectory, choosing forwards or backwards with probability one half each time, and it applies the U-turn check to every subtree as well as to the whole. The result is a set of states from which the next sample can be drawn in a way that is reversible by construction. Detail in Hoffman and Gelman (2014), section 3; Betancourt (2017) gives the geometric account.

The figure runs the naive variant from that paper, which collects the eligible states in a slice and then draws one uniformly. Production implementations use the memory-efficient progressive sampling of the same algorithm, and Stan and AdvancedHMC.jl use a multinomial version that weights states by their density instead of slicing. The tree structure is identical. The stopping rule is not. AdvancedHMC.jl uses the generalised criterion of Betancourt (2017), which compares the summed momentum of a subtree against the velocities at its ends rather than the two endpoint momenta the formula above uses. Depth is capped here at 7 doublings; Turing caps it at 10 through max_depth, and hitting that cap is a symptom of a step size far too small for the posterior.

Neal's funnel: where one step size cannot work

The geometry behind almost every divergence warning you will see

Hierarchical models produce a shape that defeats a single global step size. Neal (2003) reduced it to its simplest form. Draw a scale parameter, then draw the parameters it controls:

v ~ N(0, 32)     x | v ~ N(0, ev)

The width of the posterior in x is ev/2, so it spans several orders of magnitude across the range of v. The mouth is wide and the neck is narrow. Applying the stability condition from the previous section direction by direction, the leapfrog integrator holds together only while

ε < 2 ev/2   ⇔   v > 2 log(ε/2)    // below this height the integrator is unstable

That is a prediction with a number in it. The dotted line in the figure sits at v* = 2 log(ε/2). Divergences gather around that height, and the cloud of draws does not reach far past it. Move ε and watch the line, the red points and the bottom of the cloud travel together.

A divergence is recorded at the state the chain was in when the trajectory failed, and not at the point where the integrator broke. The sampler was sitting in the mouth of the funnel, tried to integrate down into the neck, and could not.

Two responses are on offer, and the figure shows both. Shrink the step size and the sampler reaches further down the neck, at the price of far more leapfrog steps per draw. Watch that counter climb as ε falls. Raising the target acceptance rate is the same move made automatically, since a higher target forces warmup to settle on a smaller ε. It buys depth. It does not remove the problem, because the funnel keeps narrowing without limit.

Reparametrise and the problem goes away rather than shrinking. Write v = 3zv and x = ev/2zx with zv, zx ~ N(0, 1). The sampler now explores a standard bivariate normal, which has one scale everywhere, and the funnel is recovered by transforming the draws afterwards. Switch to non-centred and the divergences vanish while the plotted cloud reaches the bottom of the neck.

Read the pattern, not the count. Divergences scattered evenly through the posterior usually mean the step size is a little too large everywhere, and a higher target acceptance rate fixes them. Divergences concentrated in one region, as here, mean the geometry itself is bad there. That is a model reparameterisation problem, and no amount of tuning will remove it.

Warmup, honestly

NUTS does not avoid burn-in. It has a default, and it discards it for you.

The mcmc session says NUTS needs "no manual burn-in", set against Metropolis-Hastings. That comparison is misleading and worth correcting, because it suggests NUTS is doing something structurally different.

NUTS discards warmup iterations like any other chain. In Turing, sample(model, NUTS(), 2000) runs 3000 iterations: min(1000, N ÷ 2) = 1000 warmup, discarded, followed by the 2000 you asked for. The warmup length is the first positional argument, so NUTS(2000, 0.65) adapts for 2000 iterations. The default has been chosen for you. It has not been dispensed with.

The course's own adaptive Metropolis run has exactly the same structure. It passes num_warmup = 5000 to RAM and discards those iterations, returning the 5000 that follow. The difference is that you typed the number.

What dual averaging is doing

Each iteration returns an acceptance statistic α, the average of min(1, e−ΔH) over the states in the trajectory. Dual averaging (Nesterov 2009, as adapted in Hoffman and Gelman 2014, section 3.2) drives the running mean of α towards a target δ:

H̄m = (1 − 1m+t0)H̄m−1 + 1m+t0(δ − αm)   // how far off target, smoothed log εm = μ − √mγH̄m   // too many rejections → smaller ε log ε̄m = m−κ log εm + (1 − m−κ) log ε̄m−1   // averaged, and used after warmup

The figure runs this for real, on a Gaussian with marginal standard deviations 1 and 0.3 and correlation 0.9, starting from a deliberately bad step size. The top panel is ε per iteration with the averaged ε̄ over it. The bottom panel is the running mean of α converging on δ. At the two marked points the mass matrix is replaced by the diagonal variance of the draws in the window just finished, which is the second job of warmup. Each replacement resets the dual averaging, which is the step you can see in ε.

Drag the warmup length down to 60 and the learned standard deviations fall well short of the truth, because a short window gives a noisy variance estimate on a correlated posterior. Give it a few hundred and they settle near 1.0 and 0.3, still carrying the wobble a finite window leaves. That is the argument against a warmup of a few dozen iterations. Divergences during warmup are ordinary while the step size is still being tuned, and Turing counts them separately from the divergences in the kept draws.

What raising δ costs. Push δ from 0.65 to 0.99 on the default settings here and the final ε falls from about 0.52 to about 0.17, while the leapfrog count per draw goes from about 5 to about 16. The trajectory has the same path to cover in smaller pieces. Raising the target acceptance rate is a decision to spend roughly three times the gradient evaluations in exchange for an integrator that survives the narrow parts of the posterior.

Turing's default target of 0.65 is close to the value derived for HMC in the high-dimensional limit by Beskos et al. (2013), which is 0.651. Stan defaults to 0.8. For comparison, the corresponding optimum for a random-walk Metropolis proposal is 0.234 (Roberts, Gelman and Gilks 1997), which is why RAM targets that instead. The three numbers are optimal for three different proposals, and are not interchangeable.

Simplified here for legibility: two estimation windows, at half and three quarters of warmup, with the first eighth thrown away. Stan and AdvancedHMC.jl use an initial fast interval, a sequence of windows that double in length, and a final interval that adjusts only the step size. The metric here is diagonal, as Turing's default DiagEuclideanMetric is, so the correlation of 0.9 is left in place. Every curve and every chip is the mean over three seeds, because one warmup is noisy enough that a single nudge of δ can move ε̄ the wrong way. Read the trend across the presets. The learned standard deviations carry the same noise, being one window's variance estimate on a correlated chain.

Common questions

The ones that come up in the practical

Does NUTS need burn-in?

Yes. It runs a warmup phase and discards it, with a default length of min(1000, N ÷ 2) in Turing, so what it saves you is the decision. You should still look at the trace plot afterwards, because a chain that is still drifting after warmup needs a longer one.

Is a divergence always fatal?

A handful out of thousands of draws, scattered rather than clustered, is usually tolerable and worth one attempt at a higher target acceptance rate. Many divergences, or a few that all sit in one region, mean the sampler is avoiding somewhere it cannot reach, so the posterior is biased. Plot the divergent draws against the parameters to see which it is.

Why does raising the target acceptance rate help?

The target is what dual averaging tunes the step size to hit, so demanding a higher acceptance rate forces a smaller ε. A smaller ε keeps the leapfrog integrator stable in narrower regions. It costs proportionally more gradient evaluations per draw, and it cannot fix a geometry that narrows without limit.

What is the mass matrix actually doing?

It rescales the momentum, which rescales the parameter space the dynamics move through. Setting M to the inverse posterior covariance makes every direction oscillate at the same rate, so one step size suits all of them. Turing estimates a diagonal version from the warmup draws, which fixes differing scales between parameters but leaves correlation between them in place.

Why can NUTS not handle discrete parameters?

The proposal is generated by integrating gradients of the log-posterior, and a density over a discrete parameter has no gradient with respect to it. In Turing you combine NUTS with a discrete sampler through Gibbs, or marginalise the discrete parameters out of the model analytically. The particle filter later in the course is the other route, and it is gradient-free for the same reason.

Why is my chain slow when R-hat and ESS look fine?

Cost per draw is the leapfrog count, which is roughly the trajectory length divided by ε, and neither appears in the convergence diagnostics. A posterior with one very narrow direction forces a small ε and long trajectories, so each draw is expensive even though the draws are good. Check the tree depth, since at the cap of 10 the sampler is taking 1023 leapfrog steps for every draw it returns.

Should I still run multiple chains?

Yes. NUTS explores a single mode well and gives no protection against a posterior with several modes. The diagnostics page works through what one chain leaves undefined.

The practical says NUTS "just works". When does it not?

Hierarchical models with a centred parameterisation, as in the funnel above, and posteriors with hard boundaries the sampler keeps hitting. Models where the gradient is expensive or inaccurate also suffer, which includes ODE systems solved to a loose tolerance, since integration error in the solver becomes noise in ∇U. All three show up as divergences or as tree depth at the cap.
