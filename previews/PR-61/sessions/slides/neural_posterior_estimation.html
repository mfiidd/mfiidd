Neural posterior estimation

Learning the posterior once, using it many times

Paying for inference twice

Where we got to

Particle MCMC gave us the posterior for the SEITL model — at a price:

  • one particle filter per likelihood evaluation
  • hundreds of thousands of evaluations per chain
  • hours of compute

And the answer applies to exactly one dataset.

The same model, again

A second island. A different reporting schedule. The same outbreak truncated at week three.

Every one of those is a fresh chain, from scratch, at full cost.

Nothing we computed the first time helps us the second time.

Variational inference got us halfway

In the previous session we replaced sampling with optimisation: pick a family \(q(\theta)\), then optimise until it is close to the posterior.

Much faster. But \(q(\theta)\) was fitted to one dataset.

Change the data and you re-run the optimisation.

What if we optimised something that works for any dataset?

Amortised inference

The idea

Stop treating the posterior as a thing to compute, and start treating it as a function to learn:

\[q_\phi(\theta \mid y) \approx p(\theta \mid y)\]

Train it on simulations, then apply it to real data.

Training data is free

We already have a simulator. Use it to generate pairs:

\[\theta_i \sim p(\theta), \qquad y_i \sim p(y \mid \theta_i)\]

Then fit a conditional density estimator by maximum likelihood:

\[\min_\phi \; -\sum_i \log q_\phi(\theta_i \mid y_i)\]

Over draws from the joint \(p(\theta)p(y \mid \theta)\), the minimiser of that loss is the true posterior (Papamakarios and Murray 2016).

So what is it, really?

Not a new model. Not a new inferential target.

The same posterior, for the same mechanistic model, with the same priors — reached by regression on simulations instead of by sampling.

The neural network replaces the sampler, not the model. \(R_0\) still means \(R_0\).

Normalising flows

We need a \(q_\phi(\theta \mid y)\) we can both evaluate (to train) and sample from (to use).

A normalising flow is an invertible network mapping a standard normal to something complicated, conditioned on the data:

\[z \sim \mathcal{N}(0, I), \qquad \theta = T_\phi(z; y)\]

Invertible with a tractable Jacobian, so the change-of-variables formula gives the density directly.

Amortisation

Training is expensive and happens once.

After that, a posterior is a single forward pass: milliseconds, no MCMC, no further simulation.

The cost moves from per dataset to once, ever. That is the whole trade.

How this differs from VI

Two kinds of optimisation

Variational inference Neural posterior estimation
Optimises \(q(\theta)\), one dataset \(q_\phi(\theta \mid y)\), all datasets
Divergence reverse KL forward KL, averaged over \(y\)
Fails by being too narrow too wide
Needs the likelihood only the simulator
New dataset re-optimise free

NPE is, in effect, amortised variational inference with the KL reversed.

Why the failure modes differ

Reverse KL is mode-seeking — it would rather cover one mode well than spread thin. Hence the too-narrow mean-field posteriors you saw.

Forward KL is mass-covering — it is penalised for putting low density where the truth has mass. Hence posteriors that err wide.

Erring wide is usually the safer failure.

The amortisation gap

The estimator must work for every dataset the prior can produce.

So on any single dataset it will be worse than a bespoke fit.

More training shrinks the gap. It does not close it.

Making it work

Ragged surveillance

Real data is not a tidy rectangle: locations start reporting at different times, days are missed, sites are surveyed twice in seven years.

But a neural network needs fixed-length input.

Fix a grid, then add a binary mask saying which points were observed (Wang et al. 2024):

\[x = [\; \underbrace{y \odot m}_{\text{zero-filled}} \;|\; \underbrace{m}_{\text{the mask}} \;]\]

Why the mask is not optional

Zero-filling alone is ambiguous.

Is a zero on day 12 a day when nobody reported, or a day when genuinely zero cases were reported?

Those imply very different things about \(\theta\). The mask is what distinguishes them — the padding carries no information at all.

One network, every schedule

Draw a random mask for every training example.

The network then learns \(p(\theta \mid y, m)\) — a posterior conditioned on the observation schedule itself.

So we can condition on schedules we invent after training. That is how you price a surveillance design without refitting anything.

Never reuse a simulation

Neural networks usually overfit a fixed training set — validation loss bottoms out, then climbs.

A SEITL outbreak takes well under a millisecond to simulate.

So: draw a fresh batch for every gradient step. No example is ever seen twice, and overfitting becomes impossible by construction.

Costs about 5% extra per step — free, but only because the simulator is fast.

What it costs

Checking the answer

There is no \(\hat{R}\), no effective sample size, no trace plot to stare at.

Instead: simulation-based calibration (Talts et al. 2018). Draw from the prior, simulate, fit, and record the rank of the truth among the posterior draws.

Calibrated \(\Rightarrow\) those ranks are uniform.

The silent failure

MCMC on a badly specified model gives you visibly bad fits and pathological diagnostics.

A trained flow will map data it has never seen anything like onto a tidy, narrow, confident and wrong posterior — with nothing to flag it (Hermans et al. 2022).

Prior and posterior predictive checks are your main guard. They matter more here than they did for MCMC.

When to use which

  • NUTS — tractable, differentiable likelihood, one dataset
  • PMMH — stochastic model, exact, one dataset, you have hours
  • VI — need speed on one dataset, can live with narrow intervals
  • NPE — same model, many datasets, or many hypothetical designs

For a one-off fit, use PMMH. For the hundredth fit of the same model, amortisation wins.
